== Runtime View

ifdef::arc42help[]
[role="arc42help"]
****
.Contents
The runtime view describes concrete behavior and interactions of the system’s building blocks in form of scenarios from the following areas:

* important use cases or features: how do building blocks execute them?
* interactions at critical external interfaces: how do building blocks cooperate with users and neighboring systems?
* operation and administration: launch, start-up, stop
* error and exception scenarios

Remark: The main criterion for the choice of possible scenarios (sequences, workflows) is their *architectural relevance*. It is *not* important to describe a large number of scenarios. You should rather document a representative selection.

.Motivation
You should understand how (instances of) building blocks of your system perform their job and communicate at runtime.
You will mainly capture scenarios in your documentation to communicate your architecture to stakeholders that are less willing or able to read and understand the static models (building block view, deployment view).

.Form
There are many notations for describing scenarios, e.g.

* numbered list of steps (in natural language)
* activity diagrams or flow charts
* sequence diagrams
* BPMN or EPCs (event process chains)
* state machines
* ...


.Further Information

See https://docs.arc42.org/section-6/[Runtime View] in the arc42 documentation.

****
endif::arc42help[]

The scenarios below cover the architecturally relevant flows of *Drop*:
the use cases that touch several building blocks, cross trust boundaries
or exercise the resilience and security concepts described in
chapter 8.

=== Scenario 1 – User Registration

Trigger:: A new passenger or driver opens the app and creates an
account.

Flow::
. *Mobile App* collects profile data and submits it to the *Backend*
  (REST/HTTPS).
. *Identity* context validates the input, creates the user and assigns
  the requested role (Passenger / Driver).
. *Identity* delegates credential storage to the external identity
  provider and receives a JWT.
. The JWT is returned to the *Mobile App* and stored in an HttpOnly,
  Secure cookie.
. *Identity* publishes a `UserRegistered` event on Kafka.
. *Analytics* consumes the event and updates `UserAnalytics`
  (`activeUsers`).

Notable aspects::
* Authentication is delegated to the external identity provider – no
  passwords are stored in Drop.
* Account creation succeeds even if Analytics is down; the event is
  processed asynchronously.

=== Scenario 2 – Search and Book a Ride

Trigger:: A passenger searches for a ride and books one of the
results.

Flow::
. *Mobile App* sends pickup, destination and desired time to the
  *Backend*.
. *Matching* queries the *Map & Routing Provider* for distance and
  ETA and returns a list of nearby available drivers.
. The passenger selects a ride; *Rides* creates a `Ride` in state
  `REQUESTED` and publishes a `RideRequested` event.
. *Notifications* pushes the request to the selected driver via
  Firebase Cloud Messaging.

Notable aspects::
* All calls to the Map & Routing Provider go through a Circuit Breaker
  (chapter 8); on open circuit the request fails fast with an
  RFC 7807 error.
* The booking call is idempotent (the client sends an idempotency
  key) so a retry on a flaky network does not create duplicate rides.

=== Scenario 3 – Driver Accepts or Declines

Trigger:: The driver receives a ride request and reacts.

Flow::
. The driver’s *Mobile App* receives the push notification and shows
  the request.
. The driver accepts or declines; the response is sent to *Rides*.
. On *accept*, the `Ride` transitions to `ACCEPTED` and a
  `RideAccepted` event is published; the *Realtime Service* opens a
  tracking channel for both apps.
. On *decline*, *Matching* selects the next eligible driver and the
  flow continues from Scenario 2, step 4.

Notable aspects::
* State transitions are guarded server-side; the client cannot push
  the ride into an arbitrary state.
* Timeouts on the driver side automatically trigger a decline so that
  the request is re-dispatched.

=== Scenario 4 – Live Tracking

Trigger:: An accepted ride is in progress.

Flow::
. The driver app reads GPS coordinates and publishes them to the
  *Realtime Service* (Firestore).
. Both apps subscribe to the Firestore document of the ride and
  receive position and status updates as a stream.
. *Rides* listens to the same stream to update the canonical ride
  state.

Notable aspects::
* The update frequency is tuned to balance battery, data use and
  perceived latency.
* If the realtime channel is interrupted, the apps fall back to
  short-interval REST polling against *Rides* until the channel
  recovers.

=== Scenario 5 – Ride Completion and Payment

Trigger:: The driver marks the ride as completed.

Flow::
. *Rides* transitions the `Ride` to `COMPLETED` and publishes
  `RideCompleted`.
. The *Payments* façade triggers the *Payment Service*, which calls
  Stripe Connect to charge the passenger, pay out the driver and
  retain the commission.
. Stripe confirms via webhook; the *Payment Service* publishes
  `PaymentSettled`.
. *Notifications* sends a receipt to the passenger and a payout
  notice to the driver.
. *Analytics* consumes `RideCompleted` and `PaymentSettled` to update
  `RideAnalytics` and `RevenueAnalytics`.

Notable aspects::
* No card data ever touches Drop – only Stripe tokens are stored
  (constraint OC1, ADR-3).
* Payment calls are idempotent; on network failure they are retried
  with exponential backoff.
* If Stripe is unavailable, the ride is still marked completed and
  the charge is queued for later settlement.

=== Scenario 6 – View Ride History

Trigger:: A user opens the history screen.

Flow::
. *Mobile App* calls *Rides* with the current JWT.
. *Rides* returns the user’s own rides (filtered by user ID extracted
  from the JWT) with date, route, fare and counterparty rating.

Notable aspects::
* Authorisation is enforced server-side; a user can only read their
  own ride records.
* The endpoint is paginated to keep response times stable as history
  grows.
